Statistical concepts often feel abstract until they are anchored in physical reality. We've built a suite of interactive components—**Dice, Coins, Urns, and Plots**—to bring these concepts to life. Here is how they work with real-world examples.

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#eab308">1. The Gambler's Fallacy (Bernoulli Trials)</CinematicText>

In a fair coin toss, every flip is independent. The coin has no memory. Yet, humans often believe that after a streak of "Heads", "Tails" is due.

**Real World Data:** In 1913, at the Monte Carlo Casino, roulette black came up **26 times in a row**. Gamblers lost millions betting on red, convinced the streak *had* to break.

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#ef4444">2. The Sample Space (Dice Rolls)</CinematicText>

A single die defines a uniform distribution $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. Two dice create a triangular distribution of sums, peaking at 7.

<div class="flex justify-center gap-12 my-8 items-center">
    <Dice value={1} size={80} color="white" dotColor="#ef4444" animate={true} />
    <span class="text-4xl font-bold">+</span>
    <Dice value={6} size={80} color="white" dotColor="#ef4444" animate={true} />
    <span class="text-4xl font-bold">= 7</span>
</div>

**Craps Odds:**
The most common roll is 7 (6 ways), while 2 (Snake Eyes) and 12 (Boxcars) are the rarest (1 way each).

| Sum | Combinations | Probability | Real World Nickname |

| **7** | (1,6), (2,5), (3,4)... | 16.67% | "Natural" |
| **12** | (6,6) | 2.78% | "Boxcars" |
</CustomTable>

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#8b5cf6">3. Sampling Without Replacement (The Urn)</CinematicText>

Imagine a lottery or raffle. Once a winning ball is drawn, it isn't put back. This changes the odds for the remaining balls. This is the **Hypergeometric Distribution**.

<div class="flex justify-center my-8">
    <div class="flex flex-col items-center">
        <Urn 
            ballCounts={{ red: 5, blue: 45 }} 
            size={250} 
            animateExtraction={true} 
        />
        <p class="mt-4 text-sm text-gray-500">A "fair" jar: 5 winning balls (Red), 45 losing (Blue).</p>
    </div>
</div>

**Real World Application (Quality Control):**
A factory produces 1000 widgets, 10 are defective. If an inspector samples 20 random widgets, what is the chance they find at least one defect? We model this exactly like drawing 'defective' balls from an urn.

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#10b981">4. The Normal Approximation</CinematicText>

When we flip a coin enough times (say, $n=100$), the discrete Binomial distribution starts to look remarkably smooth. This is the **Central Limit Theorem** in action.

<div class="flex flex-wrap justify-center gap-8 my-8">

    </div>
    <div class="text-center">
        <h3 class="mb-4">Normal Approximation</h3>
        <DistributionPlot type="normal" mean={0} sd={1} width={300} height={200} color="#3b82f6" fill={true} />
    </div>
</div>

**Insight:**
The discrete bars of the Binomial distribution (left) are perfectly approximated by the continuous Normal bell curve (right) when sample sizes are large. This allows statisticians to use Calculus to solve discrete counting problems!
