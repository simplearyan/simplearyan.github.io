Chapter 3, **Describing Categorical Data: Frequency Distribution**, focuses on the tools and methods used to organise, visualise, and summarise <StrokeUnderline color="#3b82f6">qualitative information</StrokeUnderline>. Below is a detailed breakdown of the topics, accompanied by examples and practice exercises.

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#3b82f6">1. Frequency and Relative Frequency</CinematicText>

<Concept title="Frequency Distribution">
This is a list of **distinct values (categories)** and their **frequencies** (the count of how many times each occurs).
</Concept>

<Concept title="Relative Frequency">
This is the **ratio of the frequency** of a category to the total number of observations. It is expressed as a value between 0 and 1 and is crucial for <MarkerHighlight color="purple">comparing datasets</MarkerHighlight> of different sizes.
</Concept>

    <p class="mb-4">Suppose you have the following data: <strong>A, A, B, C, A, D, A, B, D, C</strong> (Total = 10).</p>

    | B | 2 | $2/10 = 0.2$ |
    | C | 2 | $2/10 = 0.2$ |
    | D | 2 | $2/10 = 0.2$ |
    | **Total** | **10** | **1.0** |
    </CustomTable>
</div>

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#3b82f6">2. Graphical Displays</CinematicText>

<Concept title="Pie Chart" variant="minimal">
A circle divided into slices proportional to the relative frequencies. It is best used for comparing <MarkerHighlight color="orange">parts of a whole</MarkerHighlight>.
</Concept>

<Concept title="Bar Chart" variant="minimal">
Displays categories on the horizontal axis and frequencies (or percentages) on the vertical axis. The **bars should not touch**, and the height represents the count.
</Concept>

<Concept title="Pareto Chart" variant="minimal">
A specific type of bar chart where the categories are **sorted by frequency** from highest to lowest. Used to identify significant issues.
</Concept>

<Concept title="Ordinal Data Rule" variant="minimal">
If categories have a natural rank (e.g., S, M, L), the bar chart must <MarkerHighlight color="green">preserve that ordering</MarkerHighlight> rather than sorting by frequency.
</Concept>

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#3b82f6">3. The Area Principle</CinematicText>

<Concept title="The Area Principle">
The **area occupied** by a part of a graph should correspond exactly to the **amount of data** it represents. Violating this is a common way to mislead with statistics.
</Concept>

### Misleading Graphs
*   **Decorated Graphs:** Using 3D images or shapes (like bottles or boxes) often distorts the visual area compared to the data.
*   **Truncated Graphs:** When the vertical axis (baseline) does not start at zero, it can exaggerate small differences between categories.
*   **Manipulated Y-Axis:** Expanding or compressing the scale can make changes look more or less significant than they are.

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#3b82f6">4. Summarising Categorical Data</CinematicText>

<Concept title="Mode">
The **most common category** (the one with the highest frequency).

*   **Bimodal/Multimodal:** If two or more categories tie for the highest frequency.
</Concept>

<Concept title="Median">
Only applicable to **ordinal data**. It is the category of the middle observation after the data has been sorted by rank.

*   *Calculation:* If there are 15 observations, the median is the 8th value in the sorted list.
</Concept>

<Problem number="1" title="Calculating Frequencies">
A total of 2000 cases of Covid-19 were registered in 5 districts. Given the proportions below, find the relative frequency for **Nagpur** and the total cases for **Pune**.

<CustomTable style="minimal" color="red">
| District | Relative Frequency |
| :--- | :--- |
| Mumbai | 0.35 |
| Pune | 0.20 |
| Nagpur | $x$ |
| Thane | 0.25 |
| Nashik | 0.08 |
</CustomTable>

<Solution>
1.  **Finding Nagpur ($x$):** The sum of all relative frequencies must equal 1.0.
    $$0.35 + 0.20 + x + 0.25 + 0.08 = 1.0$$
    $$0.88 + x = 1.0 \implies \mathbf{x = 0.12}$$
2.  **Cases in Pune:** Multiply the total cases by the relative frequency for Pune.
    $$2000 \times 0.20 = \mathbf{400 \text{ cases}}$$
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem number="2" title="Identifying Concepts">
Which descriptive measure can be used for both nominal and ordinal data?

(a) Mean
(b) Median
(c) Mode

<Solution>
**(c) Mode.**

The **mode** is the only measure listed that applies to both nominal (labels without order) and ordinal (ranked labels) data. The median requires an order, and the mean requires numerical values.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Fruit Salad Analogy" variant="gamified">
Think of describing categorical data like **sorting a mixed bag of fruit**.

*   The **Frequency Distribution** is your final count: 10 apples, 5 bananas, 2 oranges.
*   The **Pie Chart** shows you what portion of your bag is apple-flavoured.
*   The **Mode** is simply pointing out that apples are the most common fruit you found.
*   But be careful—if you draw a picture of the fruit and make a tiny orange look as big as a giant apple just to be artistic, you have **violated the Area Principle** and misled your audience!
</Concept>
