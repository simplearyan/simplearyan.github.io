Chapter 2 of the "Stats 2 book" introduces the crucial concepts of <StrokeUnderline color="#ec4899">repeated trials and sampling</StrokeUnderline>, providing the mathematical framework for analysing sequences of experiments where the outcomes influence each other or are drawn from a limited pool. This framework builds upon the basic concepts of probability space and events discussed in Chapter 1.

The chapter primarily focuses on three major statistical distributions that arise from repeating experiments: **Bernoulli Trials** (leading to Binomial and Geometric distributions), the **Poisson Approximation**, and the **Hypergeometric Distribution** (for sampling without replacement).

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#ec4899">2.1 Bernoulli Trials: Success, Failure, and Repetition</CinematicText>

Bernoulli trials form the foundation for modelling repetitive experiments that yield a binary outcome.

### Concept Explanation: Bernoulli Trial

<Concept title="Bernoulli Trial" variant="gamified">
A single Bernoulli trial is an experiment where the outcome is classified strictly as either a **"Success"** or a **"Failure"**.

*   Let $p$ be the probability of success on any given trial.
*   The trials in a sequence are always assumed to be **independent**.
</Concept>

<CustomTable style="sketchy-colorful-outline" color="purple" font="sans">
| Experiment | Success Event Example | Probability ($p$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Toss a fair coin | Head appears | $1/2$ |
| Roll a die | Six appears | $1/6$ |
</CustomTable>

If we denote a single trial with parameter $p$ as $Bernoulli(p)$, we define the sample space $S = \{\text{success, failure}\}$ where $P(\{\text{success}\}) = p$.

### The Binomial Distribution (Counting Successes)

When you perform $n$ independent Bernoulli trials and you are interested in the **total number of successes ($k$)** observed, the resulting distribution is the **Binomial Distribution** ($Binomial(n, p)$).

<ScribbleCard scribbleStyle="neat" backPattern="grid" animationDuration="1s">
  <div slot="front">
    <h3>Binomial Probability</h3>
    <br/>
    $$P(B_k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$$
    <br/>
    <p>For $0 \le k \le n$</p>
  </div>
  <div slot="back">
    <h3>Interpretation</h3>
    <p>Probability of exactly <strong>k</strong> successes in <strong>n</strong> trials.</p>
    <p>$\binom{n}{k}$ counts the arrangements.</p>
    <p>$p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of one specific sequence.</p>
  </div>
</ScribbleCard>

#### Example Question and Concept Application (Mode)

<Problem number="1" title="Free Throw Mode">
**Question:** If a basketball player, Mark, is a $p=0.7$ free-throw shooter and attempts $n=10$ independent free throws, what is the most likely number of shots he will make?

<Solution>
**Concept Explanation (Mode):** The "mode" is the value of $k$ that makes the probability $P(B_k)$ largest. For a $Binomial(n, p)$ distribution, the most likely number of successes is the integer $k$ given by the formula: $k = \lfloor p(n+1) \rfloor$.

**Solution:**
Using $p=0.7$ and $n=10$:
$$k = \lfloor 0.7(10 + 1) \rfloor = \lfloor 0.7 \times 11 \rfloor = \lfloor 7.7 \rfloor$$
The most likely number of successful throws is **7**.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#ec4899">The Geometric Distribution (Waiting for Success)</CinematicText>

The Geometric Distribution ($Geometric(p)$) describes the probability that the **first success occurs exactly on the $k^{\text{th}}$ trial**.

<div class="flex justify-center gap-4 my-8 flex-wrap">
    <PlayingCard rank="K" suit="hearts" animation="zoom-in" duration="0.8s" delay="0.2s" flip={true} backContent="Memoryless">
        ### Geometric<br/>Prop
        
        The only discrete<br/>memoryless dist.
    </PlayingCard>
    
    <Concept title="Geometric Probability" variant="minimal">
    For the first success to occur on the $k^{\text{th}}$ trial, the first $k-1$ trials must all be failures, and the $k^{\text{th}}$ trial must be a success.
    
    $$\mathbf{P(C_k) = (1-p)^{k-1} p} \quad \text{for } k \ge 1$$
    </Concept>
</div>

**Key Property:** The Geometric distribution is **memoryless**. This means that if an event (like finding a head on a coin toss) hasn't occurred by trial $n$, the probability that it takes $m$ more trials is the same as the probability it would have taken $m$ trials from the start.

#### Example Question and Solution

<Problem number="2" title="Rolling a Six">
**Question:** A fair die is rolled repeatedly. What is the probability that the first 6 appears exactly on the fifth roll?

<Solution>
**Concept:** Success is rolling a 6 ($p=1/6$). We are looking for $k=5$.

**Solution:** The probability of failure is $1 - 1/6 = 5/6$.
$$P(\text{first 6 on roll 5}) = \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{5-1} \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{625}{7776}$$
</Solution>
</Problem>

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#ec4899">2.2 Poisson Approximation: When $n$ is Large and $p$ is Small</CinematicText>

Calculating Binomial probabilities directly becomes computationally challenging when the number of trials ($n$) is very large. The **Poisson Distribution** serves as an effective approximation in cases where $n$ is large and the probability of success ($p$) is very small, provided the product $\lambda = np$ remains constant.

<ScribbleCard scribbleStyle="messy" backPattern="dots" animationDuration="1.2s">
  <div slot="front">
    <h3>Poisson Formula</h3>
    <br/>
    $$P(\{k\}) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}$$
    <br/>
    <p>where $\lambda = np$ (Average Rate)</p>
  </div>
  <div slot="back">
    <h3>Limit Case</h3>
    <p>Approximation for Binomial when:</p>
    <ul>
        <li>$n \to \infty$</li>
        <li>$p \to 0$</li>
        <li>$\lambda = np$ constant</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</ScribbleCard>

### Example Question and Solution (Approximation)

<Problem number="3" title="Independence Day Births">
**Question:** A college has $n=1460$ students. Assuming a birthrate probability $p=1/365$, what is the probability that five or more students were born on Independence Day?

<Solution>
**Concept:** Since $n$ is large (1460) and $p$ is small ($1/365$), we use the Poisson approximation.
1. Calculate $\lambda$: $\lambda = np = 1460 \times (1/365) = 4$.
2. The problem asks for $P(X \ge 5)$, where $X \sim Poisson(4)$.

**Solution:**
It is easier to calculate the complement $1 - P(X \le 4)$.
$$P(X \ge 5) \approx 1 - \sum_{k=0}^4 P(X=k) = 1 - \left[ \frac{e^{-4} 4^0}{0!} + \frac{e^{-4} 4^1}{1!} + \frac{e^{-4} 4^2}{2!} + \frac{e^{-4} 4^3}{6} + \frac{e^{-4} 4^4}{24} \right]$$
This calculation yields an approximate probability of **0.3711631**.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h2" class="mb-6 mt-12" color="#ec4899">2.3 Sampling: With and Without Replacement</CinematicText>

The methods used for Bernoulli trials (Binomial distribution) assume independence, which implies "sampling with replacement" (i.e., selecting an item and making it available for future selection).

However, in many real-world scenarios, sampling is done **without replacement**, meaning that once an item is selected, it is removed from the population pool, and subsequent trials are therefore dependent on previous outcomes.

### The Hypergeometric Distribution (Sampling Without Replacement)

The **Hypergeometric Distribution** ($HyperGeo(N, r, m)$) models the number of successes ($k$) found in a sample when sampling without replacement from a finite population.

*   **$N$**: Total population size.
*   **$r$**: Number of individuals with the characteristic (successes) in $N$.
*   **$m$**: Sample size chosen.
*   **$k$**: Number of successes found in the sample.

#### Concept: Hypergeometric Probability

<Concept title="Hypergeometric Formula" variant="gamified">
The probability $P(\{k\})$ is calculated by counting the number of ways to select $k$ successes from $r$ available items, and $m-k$ failures from $N-r$ available items, divided by the total number of ways to choose $m$ items from $N$.

$$\mathbf{P(\{k\}) = \frac{\binom{r}{k} \binom{N-r}{m-k}}{\binom{N}{m}}} \quad \text{.}$$
</Concept>

#### Example Question and Solution (Town Residents)

<Problem number="4" title="Town Demographics">
**Question:** In a town of $N=5000$ residents, $r=1000$ are under age 18. If $m=4$ residents are selected randomly *without replacement*, what is the probability that exactly $k=2$ of them will be under 18?

<Solution>
**Solution:**
We use the Hypergeometric formula with $N=5000$, $r=1000$, $m=4$, and $k=2$. The number of non-successes is $N-r=4000$.

$$P(\{2\}) = \frac{\binom{1000}{2} \binom{4000}{2}}{\binom{5000}{4}}$$
This result is approximately **0.153592**.

**Approximation Note:**
<ArrowEmphasis color="blue">
When the sample size ($m$) is small relative to the population size ($N$), sampling without replacement gives very similar results to sampling with replacement (Binomial).
</ArrowEmphasis>

*   **Hypergeometric Result:** $\approx 0.153592$
*   **Binomial Approximation Result:** $P(k=2) = \binom{4}{2} (1/5)^2 (4/5)^2 = 0.1536$
</Solution>
</Problem>
