Vectors are <MarkerHighlight color="yellow">foundational elements of linear algebra</MarkerHighlight>. They are essential in mathematics, particularly for data science. Fundamentally, a vector can be thought of simply as a <CircleEmphasis variant="rough" color="#ef4444">list</CircleEmphasis>.

<CinematicText variant="epic" as="h2" class="text-3xl font-bold mt-8 mb-4" color="#4f46e5">Basics and Representation</CinematicText>

In practical terms, vectors often arise from <StrokeUnderline variant="zigzag" color="#f59e0b">tabular data</StrokeUnderline>. For instance, if you look at a table showing India's GDP or cricket team batting averages, a vector corresponds to a row or a column of that data.

1.  <span class="font-bold text-lg"><StrokeUnderline variant="double" color="#ec4899">Row Vector</StrokeUnderline>:</span> A list of numbers arranged horizontally, representing a row of data.
    *   *Example:* If we take the batting averages of five players against South Africa from a table, we get a row vector like $(64.35, 31.92, 33.30, 26.00, 49.87)$.
2.  <span class="font-bold text-lg"><StrokeUnderline variant="double" color="#3b82f6">Column Vector</StrokeUnderline>:</span> A list of numbers arranged vertically, representing a column of data.
    *   *Example:* The averages scored by R. Sharma against several teams can be represented as a column vector:
        <div class="flex items-center gap-4">
             <ArrowEmphasis direction="right" color="#3b82f6" text="Vertical List" variant="doodle" />
             <div>
             $$
             \begin{pmatrix} 61.33 \\ 50.44 \\ 33.47 \\ 33.30 \\ 46.25 \\ 51.42 \end{pmatrix}
             $$
             </div>
        </div>

Vectors in $R^n$ (n-dimensional real space) are lists, rows, or columns containing **$n$ real entries**, and they are equivalent to points in $R^n$.

<CinematicText variant="comic-verse" as="h3" class="text-2xl font-bold mt-6 mb-4">The Importance of Vectors</CinematicText>

Vectors are important because they allow for **arithmetic operations on these lists**.

For example, if you wanted to find the average sectoral GDP over 10 years, you could represent each year's data as a row vector. You would add these 10 row vectors coordinate-wise and then multiply the resultant vector by the scalar $\frac{1}{10}$ (or $\frac{1}{10}$ times each element) to find the average GDP per sector.

## Visual Anchors: Understanding Vectors Geometrically

<Concept title="Visual Anchor: R² Representation">
  Imagine a standard Cartesian plane (x-axis and y-axis). A key method for visualizing a vector is representing a point $(a, b)$ in $R^2$ as an **arrow joining the origin $(0, 0)$ to the point $(a, b)$**.

  The vector $(1, 2)$ is visualized by an arrow starting at the origin $(0, 0)$ and ending at the point $(1, 2)$. Similarly, the vector $(-1, -1)$ is an arrow starting at $(0, 0)$ and ending at $(-1, -1)$.
</Concept>

A vector in a physical context is defined by two fundamental properties: **magnitude (size)** and **direction**. The length of the arrow represents the magnitude, and the arrowhead indicates the direction. Examples of vectors in physics include Velocity, Acceleration, and Force. Importantly, a vector does **not** have position; it remains the same if displaced parallel to itself without changing its length.


<CinematicText variant="neon" as="h3" class="text-2xl font-bold mt-6 mb-4">Vector Operations</CinematicText>

**A. Addition:**
Vector addition is done **coordinate-wise**.
*Example:* If Arun's demands are $(3, 2)$ (3 Kg Rice, 2 Kg Dal) and Neela's demands are $(5, 6)$, their total demand vector is $(3, 2) + (5, 6) = (8, 8)$.

**B. Scalar Multiplication:**
Multiplying a vector by a **scalar** (a real number) means multiplying **each entry** of the vector by that scalar.
*Example:* If Buyer A buys the same items two days in a row, the total quantity vector can be found by scalar multiplication: $2 \cdot (8, 8, 4, 10, 4) = (16, 16, 8, 20, 8)$.

<Concept title="Visual Anchor: Vector Addition">
  Vector addition in $R^2$ can be visualized using the <StrokeUnderline variant="coil" color="#8b5cf6">head-to-tail method</StrokeUnderline> or the **parallelogram law**.

  When adding two vectors, say $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$, draw them both starting from the origin. Complete the figure to form a parallelogram. The **diagonal of the parallelogram** starting from the origin represents the resultant vector from the addition.
  
  *Example:* $(1, 2) + (2, 1) = (3, 3)$. The resultant vector $(3, 3)$ is the diagonal connecting $(0, 0)$ to $(3, 3)$.
</Concept>

## Lesson Questions

We can apply these basic rules to solve problems involving vector arithmetic. Let $A = (1, 2)$ and $B = (2, 3)$.

<Problem number={1} title="Vector Operations">
  Choose the set of correct options using the vectors <MarkerHighlight color="cyan">$A=(1, 2)$</MarkerHighlight> and <MarkerHighlight color="cyan">$B=(2, 3)$</MarkerHighlight>.

  *   Option 1: $2A$ is the vector $(2, 4)$.
  *   Option 2: $3B$ is the vector $(6, 9)$.
  *   Option 3: $A + B$ is the vector $(3, 5)$.
  *   Option 4: $A - B$ is the vector $(-1, -1)$.

  <Solution>
    **All options are correct**, based on scalar multiplication and coordinate-wise addition/subtraction.

    *   **Option 1 (Scalar Multiplication):** $2A = 2 \cdot (1, 2) = (2 \cdot 1, 2 \cdot 2) = (2, 4)$. <MarkerHighlight color="green">(Correct)</MarkerHighlight>
    *   **Option 2 (Scalar Multiplication):** $3B = 3 \cdot (2, 3) = (3 \cdot 2, 3 \cdot 3) = (6, 9)$. <MarkerHighlight color="green">(Correct)</MarkerHighlight>
    *   **Option 3 (Addition):** $A + B = (1, 2) + (2, 3) = (1+2, 2+3) = (3, 5)$. <MarkerHighlight color="green">(Correct)</MarkerHighlight>
    *   **Option 4 (Subtraction):** $A - B = (1, 2) - (2, 3) = (1-2, 2-3) = (-1, -1)$. <MarkerHighlight color="green">(Correct)</MarkerHighlight>
  </Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem number={2} title="Finding a Scalar">
  Let $A = (1, 1, 1)$ and $B = (2, -1, 4)$ be two vectors. Suppose $cA + 3B = (4, j, k)$, where $c, j, k$ are real numbers (scalars). <StrokeUnderline variant="zigzag" color="#f59e0b">Find the value of $c$</StrokeUnderline>.

  <Solution>
    We use scalar multiplication and coordinate-wise addition.
    $$cA + 3B = c(1, 1, 1) + 3(2, -1, 4)$$
    $$cA + 3B = (c, c, c) + (6, -3, 12)$$
    $$cA + 3B = (c + 6, c - 3, c + 12)$$

    We are given that $cA + 3B = (4, j, k)$. By equating the first components:
    $$c + 6 = 4$$
    $$c = 4 - 6 = -2$$
    
    <div class="flex items-center gap-4 mt-4">
      <ArrowEmphasis direction="right" text="Result" color="#ef4444" />
      <span>The value of $c$ is <CircleEmphasis variant="rough" color="#ef4444">$-2$</CircleEmphasis>.</span>
    </div>
  </Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem number={3} title="Finding Unknown Components">
  Given the result $c=-2$ from Question 2, find the <MarkerHighlight color="pink">value of $j + k$</MarkerHighlight>.

  <Solution>
    <StrokeUnderline variant="straight" color="#3b82f6">Substitute $c = -2$</StrokeUnderline> back into the resultant vector derived in Question 2:
    $$(-2)A + 3B = ((-2) + 6, (-2) - 3, (-2) + 12)$$
    $$(-2)A + 3B = (4, -5, 10)$$

    Since the resultant vector is $(4, j, k)$, we have $j = -5$ and $k = 10$.

    $$j + k = -5 + 10 = 5$$
    
    The value of $j + k$ is <CircleEmphasis variant="double" color="#8b5cf6">$5$</CircleEmphasis>.
  </Solution>
</Problem>

To summarize, vectors provide an algebraic framework for dealing with multi-dimensional data or physical quantities by treating lists of numbers as single objects that can be **scaled** (multiplied by a number) or **combined** (added coordinate-wise), often visualized as arrows in space.
